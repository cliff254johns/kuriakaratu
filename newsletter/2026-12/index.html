Page 01 · Trusts, families & governance

Trust arrangements deserve a fresh governance review

Trusts can support long-term family planning, asset stewardship, charitable purposes and continuity. They are not simply documents to sign and put away. A trust works best when its purpose, responsibilities, records and decision-making arrangements remain clear as people and circumstances change.

Start with the purpose and the people

Families and organisations should begin by identifying why a trust was created, what property or interests it is intended to hold, who is responsible for administration and who is meant to benefit. Review the trust deed and related records together. Check whether the named trustees are still available and willing to act, whether powers are understood, and whether decisions are being recorded consistently. A document that no longer reflects the practical arrangement can create uncertainty at the moment it is most needed.

Kenya's evolving trust framework

The Trust Administration Act, 2026 is now listed among Kenya's legislation. Trustees, settlors, beneficiaries and professional advisers should read the current text and check commencement, registration, transition or filing requirements that apply to the particular trust. Do not rely on a headline or a summary: the correct steps can depend on the trust's origin, structure, assets and governing documents. Avoid relying on an old checklist or an informal summary. Governance review should also consider conflicts of interest, record keeping, asset ownership evidence, reporting lines and how beneficiaries can raise questions. Where a trust holds land, shares or business interests, ensure the underlying registers and corporate or property records tell a consistent story.

Practical review questions

  • Is the trust's purpose still relevant and accurately documented?
  • Are trustee appointments, powers and decision records up to date?
  • Are asset schedules, title documents and financial records reconciled?
  • Have current legal and regulatory requirements been checked against the trust's facts?
A well-maintained trust is an ongoing governance responsibility, not a once-only paperwork exercise.

Page 02 · Privacy, technology & AI

Personal data needs disciplined handling in an AI-enabled workplace

Digital tools can help organisations communicate, serve customers and work more efficiently. But introducing automation or artificial intelligence does not remove responsibility for the personal information that enters those systems.

Map information before choosing a tool

Start by identifying what personal information is collected, where it comes from, why it is needed, who can access it and how long it is retained. Separate necessary information from material gathered simply because a form or platform makes it easy to collect. A clear purpose helps an organisation decide whether a proposed tool is suitable and what safeguards are needed.

Check vendors and cross-border handling

Before staff upload customer, employee, patient or client information to a third-party platform, establish the provider's role, contractual commitments, access controls, storage arrangements, deletion process and any onward sharing. Consider whether information may be transferred or accessed outside Kenya and what legal safeguards are required. Staff should not paste confidential case files, identity documents or sensitive personal details into public AI tools without an authorised and properly assessed process.

Organisations should also define human review. AI-generated summaries, classifications or draft communications can be inaccurate or incomplete. A responsible workflow identifies who checks outputs, how errors are corrected and when a decision must be escalated to a qualified person.

Build a practical control list

  • Document the purpose and lawful basis for each processing activity.
  • Limit access according to role and business need.
  • Train staff on safe prompts, confidential information and approved systems.
  • Review supplier terms, retention settings and security measures.
  • Maintain a process for data-subject requests and suspected incidents.

Privacy should be built into procurement and workflow design, not added only after a complaint or incident. The Office of the Data Protection Commissioner publishes guidance and information that organisations can use when reviewing their policies.

References: ODPC — Rights of a data subject · Office of the Data Protection Commissioner.

Page 03 · Land, property & transactions

Property due diligence is more than looking at a title

A property transaction may involve substantial savings, business capital or family assets. A rushed review can leave a buyer exposed to disputes, restrictions, competing interests or practical problems that were not obvious during a site visit.

Confirm the property and the parties

Begin with the exact property description and the identity and authority of the parties. Check the relevant register through the appropriate official process and compare the search results with the documents supplied. A title document should not be treated as a complete substitute for current registry checks, review of the transaction history or investigation of relevant restrictions and interests.

Match records with what exists on the ground

Where appropriate, consider survey information, boundaries, access routes, occupation, leases, rates or rent records, planning and land-use requirements, approvals and any consents required for the proposed transaction. The right checks depend on the type of property, its location, the parties and the intended use. A site inspection may reveal access or occupation issues that are not apparent from the paperwork alone.

Buyers should understand the sale agreement before paying a deposit or committing to completion dates. Pay attention to conditions, stakeholder arrangements, completion documents, default consequences, vacant possession, apportionments and the process for resolving a delay. Sellers should also confirm that they can meet the obligations and provide the documents promised.

Questions worth answering before commitment

  • Does the official register match the property and seller details?
  • Are there encumbrances, cautions, restrictions, leases or third-party rights to investigate?
  • Are boundaries, access, occupation and permitted use understood?
  • Are approvals, consents, taxes, rates and completion requirements clear?
  • Does the agreement allocate risk and explain what happens if completion is delayed?

Do not rely solely on informal assurances or screenshots. Use the appropriate official records and obtain transaction-specific advice before signing or transferring funds.

Primary reference: Kenya Law — land legislation and current laws.

Page 04 · Employment & workplace practice

Year-end is a useful time to review employment records

Clear employment records help organisations manage people fairly, respond to questions and make decisions on a reliable basis. A year-end review is a practical opportunity to identify gaps before they become difficult to reconstruct.

Check the core employment file

Review written employment particulars, role descriptions, changes to terms, leave records, attendance information, payroll records, statutory remittances and relevant correspondence. Ensure that records are accurate, appropriately restricted and stored in a way that supports retrieval when needed. Where responsibilities or reporting lines have changed, update the relevant documents rather than relying on informal conversations.

Review policies against actual practice

Policies should explain how the organisation handles conduct, discipline, grievances, performance, health and safety, working arrangements, use of devices and handling of personal information. A policy that is not communicated or applied consistently may create confusion. Managers should understand the process and know when to seek guidance before taking action.

Employers should also consider how employee information is shared with payroll providers, insurers, medical providers and other service providers. Access should be limited to what is needed and supported by appropriate safeguards. Keep retention periods under review and avoid holding sensitive records indefinitely without a clear reason.

A simple review agenda

  • Confirm that employee details and contracts are current.
  • Reconcile leave, attendance, payroll and statutory records.
  • Check that disciplinary and grievance procedures are documented.
  • Review policy communication, training and manager responsibilities.
  • Secure personnel files and review third-party access.

Where a dispute or potential claim exists, do not destroy or casually alter relevant records. Preserve material and seek advice on the appropriate next steps.

Primary reference: Kenya Law — employment and labour legislation.

Page 05 · Disputes & commercial relationships

Resolve commercial disagreements with a plan, not only a reaction

A disagreement over payment, delivery, performance or interpretation of a contract can affect cash flow and long-term relationships. The first response should preserve options and establish the facts, rather than make the dispute harder to resolve.

Read the contract and build a chronology

Collect the signed agreement, schedules, variations, invoices, delivery notes, notices and relevant communications. Prepare a neutral timeline showing what each party agreed to do, what happened, when concerns were raised and what response followed. Separate documented facts from assumptions. This helps identify the real issues and whether a contractual notice, cure period or escalation clause applies.

Consider the available routes

Depending on the agreement, the subject matter and the parties' objectives, options may include a direct commercial discussion, structured negotiation, mediation, arbitration or court proceedings. Each route has different costs, confidentiality considerations, procedural requirements, enforceability issues and implications for the ongoing relationship. A clause referring disputes to a particular forum should be reviewed before steps are taken elsewhere.

Urgency matters. A limitation period, contractual deadline, preservation concern or need for interim relief may affect the sequence of action. Do not assume that informal discussions automatically pause a legal deadline. Keep communications professional and avoid admissions or threats made without considering their effect.

Prepare for a productive first discussion

  • Identify the outcome that would resolve the commercial problem.
  • List the disputed issues and the documents supporting each position.
  • Check notice, escalation, governing-law and dispute-resolution clauses.
  • Assess the value, cost, time and relationship impact of each option.
  • Record any settlement terms accurately and confirm authority to agree.

A structured early review can help parties decide whether a negotiated solution is realistic or whether formal steps are necessary.

Primary reference: Kenya Law — arbitration and civil procedure legislation.

Page 06 · Family, succession & next steps

Family succession planning begins with clear information and open questions

Planning for the future can reduce uncertainty for families and help those responsible for an estate understand what needs attention. It is often easier to organise information calmly than to reconstruct it during a difficult period.

Prepare a reliable picture of the estate

Start with a confidential inventory of assets, liabilities, ownership records, financial accounts, business interests and important documents. Note where original records are kept and who may need to locate them. Identify whether property is held individually, jointly, through a company or another arrangement. Do not assume that the person who manages an asset is necessarily its legal owner.

Review existing arrangements

Families may need to consider wills, nominations, trust arrangements, business succession plans, powers of attorney where relevant and arrangements for dependants. The appropriate documents depend on personal circumstances, the nature of the assets and applicable law. Existing documents should be reviewed after major life events, changes in assets, family circumstances or relevant legal developments.

For business owners, succession planning should also consider who can make operational decisions, how ownership interests may pass, whether key contracts address a change in control and how records will be accessed. A plan that focuses only on assets but ignores management and continuity can leave a business vulnerable.

Practical steps for families

  • Make a secure list of assets, liabilities and ownership records.
  • Identify existing wills, trust deeds and relevant nominations.
  • Keep contact details for executors, trustees and professional advisers current.
  • Discuss business continuity and decision-making arrangements.
  • Obtain advice before transferring assets or changing ownership structures.

Do not publish sensitive family, financial or identity details in an online enquiry. Share confidential information only through an appropriate process after the firm confirms how the matter will be handled.
